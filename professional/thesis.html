A Standardized Pipeline for Automated Carotid Artery Analysis

A fully automated, modular framework for multi-domain characterization of carotid stenosis from CT angiography — integrating deep learning segmentation, morphological analysis, calcification mapping, and 3D CFD simulation.

GitHub →

Background

Carotid stenosis describes the luminal narrowing of vital arteries that supply blood to the head and neck region and affects a significant portion of the general population and ischemic stroke patients. Current practice to assess the disease relies on linear measurements of the narrowed vessel and blood velocity measurements, even though evidence for the complexity and multi-dimensionality of the disease has emerged in recent years. In addition to being too simplistic, current assessment techniques are also time-intensive and operator-dependent, introducing a level of inaccuracy and variability that significantly affects treatment decisions and clinical outcomes.

While individual aspects of carotid stenosis have been studied extensively, a comprehensive, automated, and standardized pipeline that jointly characterizes vessel morphology, calcification, and hemodynamics is missing. Morphological studies are often limited to a small subset of geometric metrics, calcification characterization rarely extends beyond volume estimation, and hemodynamic analyses are frequently performed in isolation without integration into a broader clinical assessment framework.

Pipeline Overview

The proposed pipeline is implemented as a fully automated, modular framework that takes raw CTA scans as input and produces a structured dataset of morphological, calcification, and hemodynamic features describing the carotid bifurcation and its branches. The pipeline integrates several stages, including automated vessel segmentation, standardized ROI isolation, extraction of morphological descriptors, spatial characterization and quantification of calcifications, and hemodynamic modeling through three-dimensional CFD simulations. The framework is designed to enable large-scale processing, and can be implemented as open-source software. It follows a modular design in which each stage functions as an independent component, allowing individual modules to be reconfigured or replaced without altering the overall workflow, as well as giving users choices at various stages depending on their needs and requirements.

The pipeline is implemented as a bash script that calls various Python scripts, leveraging established libraries for medical image processing, geometric analysis, and CFD pre- and post-processing. It is designed to run on standard research computing hardware with optional GPU acceleration for the segmentation and CFD stages.

Geometry Processing & ROI Isolation

The closed-surface segmentations produced by the deep learning model are first converted from NIfTI format to STL surface meshes using a marching cubes algorithm applied to the binary segmentation mask. The vmtkNetworkExtraction function is then used to generate an initial centerline representing the topology of the vascular structure, which is further processed by smoothing centerline point positions and resampling to a uniform point spacing.

The two carotid arteries are distinguished using their spatial position along the x-axis of the RAS coordinate system, where higher x values correspond to the right carotid region and lower x values correspond to the left carotid region. Within each carotid system, the bifurcation point is identified from the centerline topology by locating junction nodes with three neighboring centerline segments. To standardize the geometry across patients, branches extending superior to the bifurcation (ICA and ECA) are truncated at a distance equal to eight times the sampled radius, whereas the branch extending inferior to the bifurcation (CCA) is truncated at a distance equal to twelve times the sampled radius. The surface meshes are then clipped at these distances to produce standardized ROIs for subsequent morphological and hemodynamic analysis.

Spatial Calcification Characterization

For each carotid branch, the resulting cross-sectional circles are used to construct a volumetric tube mesh by sweeping the circles along the centerline positions. Each tube mesh is then subdivided into a grid of spatial regions to enable detailed plaque localization. Longitudinal sections correspond to 20 uniform subdivisions along the centerline direction. Angular sections represent 10 equal partitions around each cross-sectional circle. Concentric sections represent three radial partitions extending from the vessel lumen toward the vessel wall. While the longitudinal sections describe the proximal–distal position of calcification relative to the bifurcation, the concentric sections characterize the depth of calcium deposits within the vessel wall. Angular sections represent circumferential positions around the vessel cross-section.

For each spatial bin, the calcium volume is normalized by the total bin volume to produce a fractional coverage value, representing the proportion of each section occupied by calcified tissue. The combination of longitudinal, angular, and concentric indexing thus provides a three-dimensional coordinate system intrinsic to each vessel, enabling independent spatial comparisons of calcification burden across patients.

Hemodynamic Simulation

The CFD simulations represented the most computationally demanding stage of the pipeline. For the SimVascular-based pipeline, individual simulations required between 1 and 15 hours to complete on CPU-only hardware. By contrast, simulations performed using the ANSYS Fluent pipeline with GPU acceleration on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080 completed in approximately 25 minutes per case — a substantial difference in computational performance that highlights the practical advantage of GPU-accelerated solvers for large-cohort hemodynamic analysis.

Across the analyzed cases, the velocity field followed the expected pattern of acceleration through regions of narrowing and curvature and deceleration in areas of expansion. In contrast, the stenotic case showed markedly different flow behavior: velocity increased substantially within the constricted region, forming a localized high-velocity jet. Following the stenosis, the flow expanded into the downstream lumen, producing a rapid reduction in velocity magnitude. Streamline visualizations further highlighted the presence of flow recirculation zones and disturbed flow in the carotid bulb regions of all cases.

The automated pipeline was evaluated on a dataset of 111 CTA cases. As each CTA scan yields both a left and right carotid bifurcation, this corresponds to 222 carotid bifurcations available for analysis across the full dataset. Of these, 73 cases (146 carotids) completed the pipeline successfully, corresponding to an overall success rate of 65.8%. Failures occurred at several stages of the pipeline where geometric assumptions or data requirements were not satisfied. The largest number of failures occurred during morphology characterization (14 cases), primarily attributable to insufficient vessel length within the standardized ROI.

The output dataset contains 177 features per carotid (354 per patient) across morphological, calcification, and hemodynamic domains — a promising tool for more extensive carotid phenotyping and predictive modeling studies linking carotid anatomy and hemodynamics to clinical outcomes such as stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). Its modular and configurable setup allows for future improvements and user tunability with no required changes to the upstream or downstream stages of the pipeline.
